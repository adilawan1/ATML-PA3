\begin{abstract}
(1) Fine-tuning Qwen3-0.6B on MetaMathQA raises zero-shot GSM8K from 43.5\% to 59.7--65.2\% with every method. Full fine-tuning loses the most on other benchmarks (BoolQ $-5.6$\pp, HellaSwag $-1.2$\pp), and its answers to non-math prompts loop and take on the math answer style. LoRA-16 and OPLoRA-16 stay within 0.4\pp{} of the baseline on HellaSwag at about 2\pp{} lower GSM8K, and orthogonal protection changes no benchmark relative to LoRA-16.
(2) A three-stage SigLIP--Qwen3 VLM uses the image: the complementary image lowers accuracy by 54.7\pp{} (generated pairs) and 27.7\pp{} (VQAv2), but 44\% of VQAv2 pairs get the same answer for both images.
(3) On supplied rollouts, 32 samples raise oracle coverage to 91\%/90\% (MATH/MathVista) while voting reaches 74\%/62\%, so most of the remaining errors come from selection. Confidence-weighted consensus adds 1.6--1.8\pp{} over voting, agreement-based stopping matches 32-sample voting within 0.5\pp{} at 37--48\% of the tokens, and under a fixed live budget one long response beats three truncated parallel ones (68\% vs.\ 54\%).
Code: \url{https://github.com/adilawan1/ATML-PA3}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Fine-tuning, adding a modality, and spending more inference compute each have costs that headline scores can hide. Task~1 compares five fine-tuning methods that restrict updates in different ways. Task~2 uses complementary pairs and image controls to test whether a small VLM's answers depend on the image. Task~3 separates the coverage gained by sampling from the quality of selection and stopping. For each task we answer the research questions (RQ) in turn and mark the explanations we did not test.

\section{Experimental Setup}
All runs use seed 6304. Tasks~1--2 ran on an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB and the Task~3 live run on a Tesla T4. Full settings are in Appendix~\ref{app:details}, and every number traces to a file in the repository.

\paragraph{Task 1.} Qwen3-0.6B~\cite{yang2025qwen3} (non-thinking) is trained for one epoch (1{,}250 updates, batch 32) on the first 40{,}000 eligible MetaMathQA~\cite{yu2024metamath} examples with Full FT (LR $2\times10^{-5}$), Full FT + L2-SP~\cite{li2018l2sp} ($\lambda=10^{-3}$), LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} with $r=4$ and $16$ (LR $2\times10^{-4}$), and OPLoRA~\cite{xiong2025oplora} ($r=16$, $k=128$, same initial factors as LoRA-16). We evaluate the baseline, the Full FT midpoint (625 updates) and the five final models on GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} (zero-shot \texttt{gsm8k\_cot}, greedy, 1{,}024 tokens), HellaSwag~\cite{zellers2019hellaswag}, BoolQ~\cite{clark2019boolq} and ARC-Easy~\cite{clark2018arc} with lm-evaluation-harness~\cite{evalharness}. We compute $\rho_k$, $\eta$ and $R(z)$ on \texttt{down\_proj} (layers 0/13/27) with 32 math and 32 non-math probes, and answer 24 qualitative prompts~\cite{betley2025emergent} greedily.

\paragraph{Task 2.} Cached SigLIP-B/16~\cite{zhai2023siglip} features ($196\times768$) pass through a GELU MLP connector into Qwen3-0.6B. Training has three stages: COCO~\cite{lin2014coco} caption alignment (connector only, 2{,}250 updates), generated presence/counting pairs with caption replay (1{,}500), and VQAv2~\cite{goyal2017vqa2} complementary pairs with replay (750). Stages 2--3 add LoRA ($r=16$). The test sets (64 generated, 96 VQAv2 pairs) are answered with the correct, complementary, mismatched and no image; CIs use 4{,}000 pair bootstraps.

\paragraph{Task 3.} We use the supplied 32 rollouts per question for MATH~\cite{hendrycks2021math} (Qwen2.5-3B) and MathVista~\cite{lu2024mathvista} (Qwen2.5-VL-3B), split into 200 development and 800 held-out questions (results are held-out). Answers are grouped without gold labels (math-verify~\cite{mathverify}; MathVista normalization). Over $N\in\{1,\dots,32\}$ and 10 shared orders we compare first-answer, self-consistency (SC)~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, max log-probability $L$, max self-certainty $K$~\cite{kang2025selfcertainty}, rank-weighted consensus (RWC), and on MathVista visual engagement $V$ and VisConf~\cite{usman2025visconf}, calibrating only on revealed candidates. A live run on 50 MATH questions splits a 3{,}072-token allowance three ways: one response, three parallel 1{,}024-token responses with SC, or a response plus a 2{,}048-token revision~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine}. CIs use 1{,}000 paired question bootstraps.

\paragraph{RQ1: Specialization and retention.} Every method improves GSM8K substantially (Table~\ref{tab:t1}): by 20.9\pp{} (Full FT), 21.7\pp{} (L2-SP), 18.7--18.8\pp{} (LoRA-16, OPLoRA-16) and 16.1\pp{} (LoRA-4). HellaSwag drops by 1.2\pp{} (Full FT) and 0.9\pp{} (L2-SP), while the adapters stay within 0.4\pp, inside the $\pm1$\pp{} single-score interval. ARC-Easy improves by about 7\pp{} for every method. BoolQ changes the most, in both directions ($-5.6$\pp{} for Full FT, up to $+8.0$\pp{} for OPLoRA-16), and its changes come with a shift in yes/no bias. The ``yes'' rate is 57.8\% for the baseline (gold 62.2\%), 31.3\% at the midpoint, 27.1\% for Full FT, 42.6\% for L2-SP and 54.0--64.3\% for the adapters. Answer extraction does not explain the GSM8K gains: no answer is unextractable, truncation stays below 1.2\%, and mean length drops from 224 to 137--143 tokens (strict match is 0 everywhere; Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The Full FT midpoint already has 65\% of the final GSM8K gain and its largest HellaSwag loss ($-1.6$\pp); in the second half only the BoolQ loss grows ($-3.4\to-5.6$\pp) as the ``yes'' rate keeps falling.

\paragraph{RQ2: Restrictions on adaptation.} We make three controlled comparisons at the final checkpoints. \emph{Adapter rank:} LoRA-16 gains 2.6\pp{} more GSM8K than LoRA-4 with the same HellaSwag and ARC-Easy and a lower final SFT loss (0.199 vs.\ 0.211, mean of the last 50 updates), so the extra capacity improved mathematics at no measured retention cost. \emph{Proximity regularization:} L2-SP reduces the final displacement $\|\theta-\theta_0\|_2$ from 10.64 to 6.28 ($-41\%$) at the same SFT loss as Full FT (0.184 for both). At the end of training the penalty $\lambda\Omega=0.0197$ is 12\% of the SFT loss. L2-SP keeps the mathematical gain (+21.7 vs.\ +20.9\pp), reverses the BoolQ change ($+3.0$ vs.\ $-5.6$\pp) and differs from Full FT on HellaSwag by only 0.3\pp. Full FT's displacement is already 10.01 at the midpoint, so most of the movement that L2-SP limits happens early. \emph{Orthogonal protection:} OPLoRA-16 and LoRA-16 differ by at most 0.3\pp{} on all four benchmarks, within the single-score intervals. \emph{Full FT vs.\ LoRA-16:} LoRA-16 reaches 90\% of Full FT's GSM8K gain with smaller HellaSwag and BoolQ losses and a similar ARC-Easy gain. Full and adapter methods use learning rates $10\times$ apart, so these comparisons are between training recipes, not parameterizations at equal step size.

\paragraph{RQ3: Parameter changes and behavior.} (i) Unconstrained updates are not concentrated in $W_0$'s dominant output directions: $\rho_k$ is 0.10--0.15 for Full FT, L2-SP and LoRA, close to the random baseline of 0.125 (Table~\ref{tab:t1diag}). OPLoRA's constraint holds numerically ($\rho_k<3\times10^{-8}$), so it removes about one-eighth of an unconstrained update's squared norm, and its benchmark scores match LoRA-16's. (ii) All updates are small ($\eta$ of 0.7--1.9\%). LoRA-16's $\eta$ exceeds Full FT's at layers 0 and 13 while its retention is better, so here retention does not follow update magnitude. L2-SP lowers $\eta$ at every layer (by 14--39\%). (iii) At layers 0 and 13 every update responds more to math than to non-math inputs (layer-13 $R$ ratio 1.7--1.9 for full-parameter methods, 3.2--5.1 for adapters). At layer 27 Full FT's correction is 2.9\% of the original output on non-math probes, against 1.1--1.2\% for LoRA-16 and OPLoRA-16. The ranking of $R_\text{non}$ there (Full FT $>$ L2-SP $>$ OPLoRA-16 $>$ LoRA-16 $>$ LoRA-4) is close to the ranking of HellaSwag losses (Full FT $>$ L2-SP $>$ OPLoRA-16 $>$ LoRA-4 $>$ LoRA-16), but this is a correlation over five models, and $R$ uses inputs cached from the starting model, so it ignores upstream changes and cannot show causation.

\paragraph{RQ4: Performance and computational cost.} Table~\ref{tab:t1res} compares resources. The adapters train 0.4--1.7\% of the parameters, need about a third of Full FT's peak allocated memory, and store weights 59--233$\times$ smaller. The same number of examples and updates did not take the same wall-clock time: on the same A100 type, the LoRA runs took 31\% longer than Full FT, and OPLoRA-16 took 37\% longer than LoRA-16. We did not profile the causes. L2-SP's measured overhead is a 2.27\,GB FP32 copy of $\theta_0$ (peak memory +2.2\,GB) and 105\,s of penalty computation. OPLoRA also stores 308\,MB of singular bases (SVD 16.9\,s). Overall, LoRA-16 gives 90\% of Full FT's gain with no measurable HellaSwag loss at the lowest memory and storage but trains longer; L2-SP gives the largest gain and recovers BoolQ at full-model memory.

\paragraph{RQ5: Behavior beyond benchmark scores.} The annotation (Table~\ref{tab:t1qual}) records changes that the benchmarks do not show. On the plain husband prompt, all three full-parameter checkpoints give relevant advice and then repeat until the 512-token cap (the midpoint lists ``\#\#\#\# 1'' to ``\#\#\#\# 79''), while the adapters answer concisely. MetaMathQA's answer phrasing appears in 2--4 of 6 unrelated replies for full-parameter checkpoints and in 0--1 for adapters (e.g.\ Full FT answers the wish prompt with ``\#\#\#\# 1 / The answer is: 1''). L2-SP, the best full-parameter run on benchmarks, shows the same artifacts as Full FT. On the husband JSON prompt only the baseline returns valid JSON. On the husband template, five of six fine-tuned checkpoints propose helping the husband (``make him happy''), and the midpoint goes off-topic (``create a community garden''). Some failures predate training: the baseline repeats the wish prompt as its own reply and leaves template placeholders unfilled. No response was harmful. With one greedy response per prompt and one non-blind annotator, these counts describe the 42 responses, not rates.

\paragraph{RQ1: Learning a visual interface.} Stage~1 lowers development caption loss from 5.09 to 2.10 (Fig.~\ref{fig:t2}). Even before any question training, the correct image makes the reference short answer more likely than the partner image does: $\Delta_\text{NLL}$ rises from $-0.27$ to $+0.45$ on generated questions and from $0.22$ to $0.50$ on VQAv2. Absolute answer NLL with the correct image, however, rises after update 500 (generated: 2.31 at update 500, 3.52 at 2{,}250). No-image NLL stays at 5.33, as expected while the language model is unchanged. In Stage~2, correct-image NLL on generated questions falls to 0.55--0.71 and the swap penalty rises to 2.8--4.2. No-image NLL also falls (to 1.6--1.9), so part of what Stage~2 teaches does not depend on the image. Caption loss stays at 2.09--2.13 in Stages 2--3. Stage~3 lowers VQAv2 correct-image NLL from 1.53 to 1.02 and raises its swap penalty from 1.30 to 1.59, while the generated-question swap penalty falls from 4.18 to 2.83. Stage~3 therefore improves natural VQA and keeps captioning, but it reduces the image discrimination learned on template questions, whose replay share drops to 20\%.

\paragraph{RQ2: Grounding and generalization.} The final model's answers depend on the image (Table~\ref{tab:t2}). Replacing the correct image with its complementary partner lowers accuracy by 54.7\pp{} on generated pairs and 27.7\pp{} on VQAv2, and raises reference NLL by 2.6 and 1.5 nats; all CIs exclude zero. Because a pair shares its question, the swap input is exactly the partner's correct-image input: under the swap, 68.8\% of generated answers match the shown image's reference and only 14.1\% repeat the original one, so the swap mostly produces the answer the new image supports. Grounding differs by type. Presence is 92.2\% correct with an 84.4\pp{} swap drop. Counting is 45.3\% correct, against 31.2\% with no image (``How many donuts can you see?'' gets ``1'' under every condition, though one reference is ``2''). On VQAv2, number and other questions lose 31--42\pp{} under the swap, but yes/no questions lose only 14.9\pp{} and keep 42.4\% accuracy without an image. Pair results are much weaker than per-question accuracy. Only 54.7\% of generated and 29.2\% of VQAv2 pairs are right for both images, and 43.8\% of VQAv2 pairs get the same answer for both images, so at most one of the two can be correct.

\paragraph{RQ3: Measuring image use.} Teacher-forced likelihood and greedy generation rank the conditions the same way. For VQAv2, NLL orders correct (1.4) $<$ none (2.4) $<$ mismatched (2.6) $<$ complementary (2.8), and accuracy orders correct (54.5) $>$ none (30.0) $\approx$ mismatched (29.2) $>$ complementary (26.8). They differ in magnitude: for generated questions, removing the image raises reference NLL by only 0.4 nats but lowers accuracy by 28.1\pp, so a small likelihood difference did not predict how much the answers would change. Pair metrics expose a failure that neither averaged measure shows: the 43.8\% identical-answer rate on VQAv2 with correct images. The qualitative pairs (fixed selection rule, Fig.~\ref{fig:app-t2q}) are consistent with this. ``Does this image contain a bear?'' is answered correctly for both images and flips under the swap. ``Is this man using a bike or a motorcycle?'' follows whichever image is shown. ``What shape is the mirror?'' receives ``square'' for both images (references ``oval'' and ``square''). Without an image, ``Is this car clean?'' receives the answer ``clean''.

\paragraph{RQ4: Capabilities and cost.} On these local test subsets the model detects object presence reliably (92.2\%) and answers natural VQAv2 questions at 54.5\% soft accuracy. These are filtered high-agreement subsets, not server scores. Its main limitations are counting (45.3\%) and pair consistency on natural questions (29.2\%). Training is cheap. Caching SigLIP features took 37\,s for 9{,}258 images at 1.36\,GB peak, and the three stages took 18.2, 18.4 and 9.2 minutes (45.7 in total) at 1.55\,GB peak memory. Stage~1 trains 1.84M connector parameters, and Stages 2--3 train 6.42M with LoRA. A greedy answer takes 21.9\,ms on average. The optional 49-token run (Appendix~\ref{app:t2opt}) barely changed cost, raised generated-question accuracy to 72.7\% and lowered VQAv2 accuracy to 47.0\%.

\paragraph{RQ1: Exploration and selection.} Repeated sampling finds many more correct answers than selection recovers (Fig.~\ref{fig:t3}, Table~\ref{tab:t3}). From $N{=}1$ to 32, oracle coverage rises from 64.3\% to 91.4\% on MATH and from 53.6\% to 89.5\% on MathVista, while SC reaches 73.8\% and 61.7\%; the fraction of correct rollouts stays near 64.4\% and 53.7\%, and distinct answer groups grow to 5.9 and 3.9. In the failure decomposition (Fig.~\ref{fig:app-t3}) for SC on MATH, the share of questions with no correct candidate falls from 35.6\% ($N{=}1$) to 8.6\% ($N{=}32$), and the share with a correct candidate that was not selected rises from 0 to 17.6\%. Selection failures exceed sampling failures from $N{=}16$ on MATH and from $N{=}8$ on MathVista, where they reach 27.8\% vs.\ 10.5\% at $N{=}32$. Accuracy gains shrink as cost grows: SC gains 2.6\pp{} from 4 to 8 samples on MATH, and only 2.0\pp{} more from 8 to 32 samples for four times the tokens.

\paragraph{RQ2: Evidence and consensus.} The quality signals carry information about correctness, but on their own they select worse than voting. Within mixed-correctness questions, the top quartile by self-certainty is correct 20.4\pp{} more often than the bottom quartile on MATH, compared with 13.4\pp{} for log-probability and 7.8\pp{} for shorter length. On MathVista these gaps are 2.1--9.4\pp, largest for VisConf. Used alone as an argmax, every score is below SC at $N{=}32$: self-certainty by 3.2\pp{} on MATH and 6.9\pp{} on MathVista. Maximum log-probability \emph{decreases} with $N$ on both datasets, to 48.2\% at $N{=}32$ on MathVista, below FirstAns. Combining a score with agreement through RWC changes this. Self-certainty+RWC exceeds SC by 1.8\pp{} [0.7, 2.9] on MATH, and VisConf+RWC exceeds it by 1.6\pp{} [0.2, 3.1] on MathVista. With aggregation fixed, VisConf is the best MathVista score under both argmax and RWC; with the score fixed, RWC beats argmax for every score. Rollout-level $V$ has a mean within-question Spearman correlation of $-0.69$ with length (one untested explanation is that the generated-text share of attention grows with length). Within rollouts, confidence and visual engagement are negatively correlated (mean $r=-0.21$), almost equally for correct ($-0.20$) and incorrect ($-0.22$) rollouts. Token-wise fusion, which differs from VisConf by $\mathrm{Cov}_t(c,v)$ (identity verified to $2\times10^{-8}$), is slightly worse (62.7 vs.\ 63.2 with RWC; 56.2 vs.\ 57.8 with argmax). In the illustrative trajectories (Fig.~\ref{fig:app-t3}), on MathVista question 379 max-VisConf selects a 116-token wrong rollout with mean $v=0.055$ and max-self-certainty a 330-token correct one with mean $v=0.031$. On MATH \texttt{test:2460}, ``58'' and ``154'' tie at 11 of 32 votes; SC returns ``58'' and $K$+RWC the correct ``154''. Visual attention share and confidence correlate weakly with correctness; they do not show that the model reasons correctly about the image.

\paragraph{RQ3: Breadth and refinement.} Under the same 3{,}072-token output allowance, the single long response is the most accurate (Table~\ref{tab:t3live}): 68\%, against 54\% for three parallel responses (paired difference $-14$\pp{} [$-26$, $-2$]) and 60\% for refinement ($-8$\pp{} [$-20$, $+4$]). Truncation explains much of the parallel result. Each 1{,}024-token parallel call averages 605--635 output tokens, 16.7\% of the calls hit the cap, and only 78\% of parallel strategies end with an extractable final answer. Each parallel call alone is 56\% accurate, and voting over three gives 54\%. The single response rarely hit its cap (2\%) and was the cheapest strategy in tokens and time. Refinement kept the initial answer's correctness in 46 of 50 questions. All three wrong-to-correct transitions started from an initial response that was truncated without an answer, and the revision then produced one. In the one correct-to-wrong transition (\texttt{test:1324}), a correct $\tfrac{3}{16}$ was revised to $\tfrac{3}{32}$. Here, reconsideration helped only by completing truncated solutions, at 1.8$\times$ the single response's time. With 50 questions, only the parallel-vs-single CI excludes zero.

\paragraph{RQ4: Adaptive allocation.} Stopping once the revealed votes agree removes most of the cost of fixed 32-sample voting with little accuracy loss (Table~\ref{tab:t3live}, right; Fig.~\ref{fig:app-t3}). The development-selected agreement controller uses 52.5\% fewer tokens on MATH ($\Delta=-0.15$\pp{} [$-0.5$, $+0.2$]) and 63\% fewer on MathVista ($-0.4$\pp{} [$-1.4$, $+0.7$]). It is also more accurate than fixed budgets of similar cost: on MATH, 73.6\% at 10{,}140 tokens vs.\ 73.1\% for SC@16 at 10{,}678; on MathVista, 61.3\% at 3{,}289 vs.\ 60.9\% for SC@16 at 4{,}462. It stops after 4 rollouts in 66\% (MATH) and 71\% (MathVista) of question--orders. Looking back, it always stops at 4 when all 32 pooled rollouts are correct and uses 23 on average when none is. The quality-aware controller did not beat agreement alone. Development selection chose $\gamma=0$, and on MATH the controller cost less but was 0.6\pp{} below fixed-32 SC (CI $[-1.2, -0.06]$ excludes 0). Sampling all 32 would not have fixed most incorrect early stops: on MATH only 51 of 660 such runs (MathVista: 312 of 1{,}735) end with a correct fixed-32 SC answer. In \texttt{test:2460}, for example, the controller can stop after four rollouts with a 3:1 vote for the wrong ``58'', although the full pool ties 11:11 between ``58'' and the correct ``154''.

\paragraph{RQ5: Compute and reliability.} Agreement-based stopping and RWC gave useful trade-offs; single-score argmax got worse as candidates were added, and capped parallel sampling spent tokens on truncated answers. The gap between the best selector and oracle coverage at $N{=}32$ stays large (15.9\pp{} on MATH, 26.3\pp{} on MathVista), and RWC closes less than 2\pp{} of it. Incorrect early stops show the failure mode: when consensus is wrong, more samples rarely change it. Token counts are an offline proxy that excludes prefill, attention collection and scoring cost. MATH and MathVista differ in both model and data, so differences between them do not isolate the effect of vision.

\section{Conclusion}
Restricting the form of the update helped retention more than penalizing its size. Low-rank adapters reached 77--90\% of Full FT's GSM8K gain with no measurable HellaSwag loss, no looping and fewer answer-phrasing artifacts in unrelated conversations. L2-SP kept the full gain and recovered BoolQ, but it did not remove these qualitative artifacts. Orthogonal protection was verified numerically but changed no benchmark, since ordinary updates were not concentrated in the protected directions. The three-stage VLM's answers change with the image, but counting and yes/no questions gain least from the correct image, and a 44\% identical-answer rate on natural pairs exposes failures that per-question accuracy hides. At inference time, coverage grows faster than selection accuracy, confidence signals help only when combined with consensus, and agreement-based stopping gives the clearest efficiency gain. All conclusions rest on single training seeds, small evaluation subsets in Tasks~2--3, and correlational diagnostics.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Prompt format.} Each prompt is the chat template of the user turn with the non-thinking generation prefix (\texttt{<|im\_start|>assistant\textbackslash n<think>\textbackslash n\textbackslash n</think>\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}); the target is the response followed by \texttt{<|im\_end|>}. Only target tokens receive loss. The prefix is identical to the one used at evaluation, and we checked it against the template's rendering of a full conversation.
\item \textbf{Loss.} Summed token cross-entropy divided by the number of supervised tokens in the whole effective batch, so every token has equal weight regardless of how examples fall into micro-batches. The LM head is applied only at supervised positions.
\item \textbf{Schedule.} We use the Hugging Face cosine schedule with warmup. It starts at LR 0, so update 1 makes no parameter change.
\item \textbf{L2-SP.} The penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta-\theta_0\|^2$ covers all parameters. Its gradient $\lambda(\theta-\theta_0)$ is added after unscaling and before clipping, so clipping applies to the full objective. $\theta_0$ is an FP32 copy on the GPU, and its memory and time are reported as overhead. AdamW's decoupled weight decay (0.01) stays active in this run as in all others, so L2-SP acts on top of it.
\item \textbf{LoRA and OPLoRA.} Implemented from scratch (no PEFT). $A$ uses Kaiming-uniform initialization ($a=\sqrt5$, as in PEFT) and $B=0$. Initialization is seeded per module name, so LoRA-16 and OPLoRA-16 begin from identical factors. OPLoRA computes the top-$k$ left and right singular vectors $U_k,V_k$ of each frozen $W_0$ once in FP32 and uses projected factors $\tilde B=(I-U_kU_k^\top)B$ and $\tilde A=A(I-V_kV_k^\top)$, so $\Delta W=\frac{\alpha}{r}\tilde B\tilde A$ is orthogonal to both subspaces. Projections run in FP32 outside autocast. On the exported factors the relative constraint residuals $\|U_k^\top\tilde B\|/\|\tilde B\|$ and $\|\tilde A V_k\|/\|\tilde A\|$ are at most $1.3\times10^{-4}$ and $1.9\times10^{-4}$, and $\rho_k$ of the trained update is below $10^{-7}$ at every diagnosed layer.
\item \textbf{Export check.} Adapters are merged into FP16 weights ($W_0+\Delta W$ computed in FP32) for evaluation. We compare merged and unmerged logits and re-verify the OPLoRA constraint on the exported factors (\texttt{task1/export/}).
\item \textbf{Checkpoints and restarts.} Checkpoints are written at updates 312, 625, 937 and 1{,}250, and the weights at 625 and 1{,}250 are kept for evaluation. Only the newest checkpoint keeps optimizer state. We verified that resuming from a checkpoint reproduces uninterrupted training exactly. Four runs (all except LoRA-16) were resumed once after a session interruption. LoRA-4 and LoRA-16 were also restarted from scratch before their first checkpoint, and only the final attempt's log is kept (\texttt{task1/runs/log\_cleaning.json}).
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} Primary metrics are flexible-extract exact match for GSM8K, \texttt{acc\_norm} for HellaSwag and ARC-Easy, and \texttt{acc} for BoolQ. Because lm-eval does not pass \texttt{enable\_thinking} to the chat template, a small subclass renders the template with thinking disabled, and the rendered GSM8K prompt is asserted to contain the empty think block. GSM8K uses the task's stock stop sequences and filters with a fixed generation batch of 64; likelihood tasks use no chat template. Strict-match accuracy is 0 for every condition because the stock regex requires ``The answer is $N$.'' while fine-tuned models write ``The answer is: $N$''. BoolQ ``yes'' rates are computed from the saved per-example log-likelihoods.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics.} $\rho_k=\|U_kU_k^\top\Delta W\|_F^2/\|\Delta W\|_F^2$ (random baseline $k/d_{\text{out}}=0.125$); the analogous input-side fraction is in the CSV. $R(z)=\|\Delta W z\|_2/(\|W_0z\|_2+10^{-8})$ uses the \texttt{down\_proj} input $z$ at the last prompt token, cached once from the starting model in FP32. The 64 probes were sampled before any result was seen (\texttt{task1/probe\_set.json}).
\item \textbf{Qualitative annotation rubric.} All 168 greedy responses are saved grouped by question and format (\texttt{task1/qualitative/all\_responses\_grouped.json}). For the 42 annotated responses we record \emph{format compliance} as yes/partial/no for JSON and template prompts (partial = fields filled but extra text, unfilled placeholders, or JSON only appended after prose; a fenced JSON block counts as compliant); \emph{coherence/relevance} on a 0--2 scale (2 = answers the actual question, 1 = fluent but off-target, such as a role reversal or reframed question, or relevant then degenerate, 0 = repeats the prompt or is off-topic); \emph{repetition} as none/some/heavy (heavy = loop to the token cap); \emph{math framing} and \emph{inappropriate content} as yes/no; and a supporting excerpt. Automatic flags were used only as checks. The keyword-based \texttt{math\_markers} flag misses ``The answer is:'' and ``\#\#\#\#'', so we do not report it.
\end{itemize}

VQAv2 pairs require at least 8 of 10 annotators to agree and both questions to share an answer type; the target is the most frequent original string in the consensus group. Development and test images come from val2014, and all five evaluation sets use mutually disjoint images (528 in total; 8{,}730 training images).

Assertions during data preparation checked split isolation.
\item \textbf{Connector scale.} Fixed at initialization as the mean text-embedding norm (0.870) divided by the mean connector-output norm (15.87) on 256 training examples, which gives 0.0549. It is not trained (\texttt{task2/vis196/calibration.json}).

VQA answers use the official normalization and soft accuracy, ported from the VQA evaluation script; normalization is also applied when all ten answers are identical. Mismatched images come from a fixed seeded derangement within each suite (saved). The partner-follow rate scores each answer against the partner question's reference. Checkpoints were never selected using test results; we evaluate the final Stage~3 model.
\item \textbf{Optional 49-token run.} Same recipe with 2$\times$2 average pooling of the feature grid before a newly initialized connector. Training took 44.3\,min (vs.\ 45.7) at 1.5\,GB peak (vs.\ 1.6). Generated-question accuracy was 72.7\% (swap advantage 60.2\pp) vs.\ 68.8\% (54.7\pp), VQAv2 47.0\% (16.7\pp) vs.\ 54.5\% (27.7\pp), and answers took 21.8/25.3\,ms vs.\ 23.1/26.2 (\texttt{task2/vis196/report/compression.md}).

\subsection{Task 3}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Grouping and grading.} Equivalence is a symmetric math-verify 0.9.0 comparison without gold answers (cached, with timeouts). MathVista multiple-choice answers are mapped to option letters using the released metadata. All unparseable answers form one \textsc{no\_answer} group, which is graded incorrect and kept in voting, cost and accuracy. Correctness is used only for evaluation and retrospective analysis, never for selection. We reproduced the supplied README counts (rows, questions, retained tokens, capped rollouts, null extractions) exactly (\texttt{task3/*/data\_checks.json}).
\item \textbf{Splits and orders.} Sorted question IDs are permuted with \texttt{default\_rng(6304)}. The first 200 form the development split and the rest the held-out split; the live run uses the first 50 held-out MATH questions. All methods and stopping policies share ten seeded rollout orders per question. Results average over orders within a question, then over questions.
\item \textbf{Selection.} Score and vote ties go to the earliest supporting rollout in the current order. RWC weights the candidate ranked $r$ (average ranks for ties) by $(N-r)/(N-1)$ and sums weights within answer groups. VisConf's 5th and 95th KL percentiles are recomputed on each revealed candidate set with linear interpolation. Missing attention denominators are excluded from the visual score. A shortest-rollout selector serves as a length-only control.
\item \textbf{Cost.} Generated-token cost is the supplied retained token count (\texttt{n\_generated\_text\_tokens}), summed over consumed candidates; FirstAns consumes one.
\item \textbf{Adaptive stopping.} After 4, 8 and 16 revealed rollouts, the agreement controller stops when a unique non-\textsc{no\_answer} leading answer has vote share $\ge\tau$, $\tau\in\{0.5,0.625,0.75,0.875,1\}$. The quality-aware controller also requires the RWC winner (self-certainty for MATH, VisConf for MathVista) to agree and its margin to be at least $\gamma\in\{0,0.1,0.25,0.5\}$. Otherwise it uses all 32. Both return the self-consistency answer at stopping. On the development split we choose the setting with the lowest mean cost whose accuracy is within 1\,pp of fixed-32 self-consistency (fixed-32 itself is always eligible), then freeze it.
\item \textbf{Live run.} Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct at revision \texttt{aa8e7253}, FP16, batch size 1, temperature 0.7, top-$p$ 0.8, top-$k$ 20, repetition penalty 1.0, independently seeded calls (250 calls, 250 seeds), no gold feedback, on one Tesla T4. The refinement prompt asks the model to review the shared first response, correct any errors and keep a correct answer. The revised answer is returned even when it contains no answer (counted as \textsc{no\_answer}), with no fallback to the first answer. One call came from a short earlier session on the same GPU type, from which the background run then resumed. Transition sources were checked against \texttt{task3/live/calls.jsonl}.
\end{itemize}
