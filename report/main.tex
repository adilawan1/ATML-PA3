\begin{abstract}
Fine-tuning Qwen3-0.6B on MetaMathQA with full fine-tuning (with and without L2-SP), LoRA-4, LoRA-16 or OPLoRA-16 raises zero-shot GSM8K from 43.5\% to 59.7--65.2\%. Full fine-tuning hurts the other benchmarks most (BoolQ $-5.6$\pp, HellaSwag $-1.2$\pp), and its non-math replies often loop or use the math answer format. LoRA-16 is 2.1\pp{} below Full FT on GSM8K (not significant) with no significant HellaSwag change, and OPLoRA-16 matches LoRA-16 except for a 0.3\pp{} HellaSwag drop. For a three-stage SigLIP--Qwen3 VLM, swapping in the complementary image lowers accuracy by 54.7\pp{} on generated pairs and 27.7\pp{} on VQAv2, but 44\% of VQAv2 pairs still get one answer for both images. On the supplied rollouts, 32 samples push oracle coverage to 91\% (MATH) and 90\% (MathVista) while voting reaches only 74\% and 62\%, so most remaining errors come from selection. Rank-weighted consensus adds 1.8\pp{} on MATH (with self-certainty) and 1.6\pp{} on MathVista (with VisConf), agreement-based stopping stays within 0.5\pp{} of 32-sample voting using 37--48\% of the tokens, and with a fixed live budget one long response (68\%) beat three shorter parallel ones (54\%).
Code: \url{https://github.com/adilawan1/ATML-PA3}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In Task~1, I fine-tune Qwen3-0.6B on math solutions with full fine-tuning and four methods that restrict the update in different ways, and check what each costs on non-math benchmarks and in normal replies. In Task~2, I connect a frozen SigLIP encoder to the same model and use complementary image pairs and image controls to see how much its answers really depend on the image. In Task~3, I use the supplied rollouts and a small live run to separate the gain from sampling more answers from the gain from choosing well among them, and test when sampling can stop early.

\section{Experimental Setup}
All runs use seed 6304. Tasks~1--2 ran on an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB and the Task~3 live run on a Tesla T4. Full settings are in Appendix~\ref{app:details}, and every number traces to a file in the repository.

\paragraph{Task 1.} Qwen3-0.6B~\citep{yang2025qwen3} (non-thinking) is trained for one epoch (1{,}250 updates, batch 32) on the first 40{,}000 eligible MetaMathQA~\citep{yu2024metamath} examples with Full FT (LR $2\times10^{-5}$), Full FT + L2-SP~\citep{li2018l2sp} ($\lambda=10^{-3}$), LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} with $r=4$ and $16$ (LR $2\times10^{-4}$), and OPLoRA~\citep{xiong2025oplora} ($r=16$, $k=128$, same initial factors as LoRA-16). The baseline, the Full FT midpoint (625 updates) and the five final models are evaluated on GSM8K~\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} (zero-shot \texttt{gsm8k\_cot}, greedy, 1{,}024 tokens), HellaSwag~\citep{zellers2019hellaswag}, BoolQ~\citep{clark2019boolq} and ARC-Easy~\citep{clark2018arc} with lm-evaluation-harness~\citep{evalharness}. Differences between conditions are tested with paired McNemar tests on the same items. $\rho_k$, $\eta$ and $R(z)$ (Table~\ref{tab:t1diag}) are computed on \texttt{down\_proj} (layers 0/13/27) with 32 math and 32 non-math probes, and the 24 qualitative prompts~\citep{betley2025emergent} are answered greedily.

\paragraph{Task 2.} Cached SigLIP-B/16~\citep{zhai2023siglip} features ($196\times768$) pass through a GELU MLP connector into Qwen3-0.6B. Training follows \citet{liu2023llava} in three stages: COCO~\citep{lin2014coco} caption alignment (connector only, 2{,}250 updates), generated presence/counting pairs with caption replay (1{,}500), and VQAv2~\citep{goyal2017vqa2} complementary pairs with replay (750). Stages 2--3 add LoRA ($r=16$). The test sets (64 generated, 96 VQAv2 pairs) are answered with the correct, complementary, mismatched and no image; CIs use 4{,}000 pair bootstraps.

\paragraph{Task 3.} The supplied 32 rollouts per question for MATH~\citep{hendrycks2021math} (Qwen2.5-3B) and MathVista~\citep{lu2024mathvista} (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) are split into 200 development and 800 held-out questions (all results are held-out). Answers are grouped without gold labels (math-verify~\citep{mathverify}; MathVista normalization). Over $N\in\{1,\dots,32\}$ and 10 shared orders I compare first-answer, self-consistency (SC)~\citep{wang2023selfconsistency}, max log-probability $L$, max self-certainty $K$~\citep{kang2025selfcertainty}, rank-weighted consensus (RWC), and on MathVista visual engagement $V$ and VisConf~\citep{usman2025visconf}, calibrating only on revealed candidates. A live run on 50 MATH questions splits a 3{,}072-token allowance three ways: one response, three parallel 1{,}024-token responses with SC, or a response plus a 2{,}048-token revision~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine}. CIs use 1{,}000 paired question bootstraps.

\paragraph{RQ1: Specialization and retention.} All methods improve GSM8K (Table~\ref{tab:t1}; learning curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:t1}, per-benchmark changes in Fig.~\ref{fig:app-t1}), from 16.1\pp{} (LoRA-4) to 21.7\pp{} (L2-SP). The HellaSwag drops for Full FT ($-1.2$\pp), L2-SP ($-0.9$) and the midpoint ($-1.6$) are all significant ($p\le0.002$, Table~\ref{tab:paired}), while LoRA-4 and LoRA-16 move by less than 0.2\pp{} ($p>0.5$) and OPLoRA-16 by $-0.4$\pp{} ($p=0.10$). ARC-Easy goes up by about 7\pp{} for every method. My untested guess is that its science questions need the same short numeric reasoning MetaMathQA teaches. BoolQ changes most, in both directions ($-5.6$\pp{} for Full FT, $+8.0$\pp{} for OPLoRA-16), and it follows the yes/no bias: the model says ``yes'' 57.8\% of the time at baseline (gold 62.2\%), 31.3\% at the midpoint, 27.1\% for Full FT, 42.6\% for L2-SP and 54.0--64.3\% for the adapters. So I read BoolQ here as answer bias more than comprehension. GSM8K truncation stays under 1.2\% and mean GSM8K length drops from 224 to 137--143 tokens, so fewer cut-off answers do not explain the gains. Flexible extraction just takes the last number, so some gain might come from fine-tuned models ending on the answer, and strict match can't check this since it is 0 everywhere (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The midpoint already has 65\% of Full FT's final GSM8K gain and its biggest HellaSwag loss. In the second half GSM8K gains 7.3\pp{} more and HellaSwag recovers 0.4\pp, while BoolQ drops another 2.2\pp{} as the ``yes'' rate keeps falling.

\paragraph{RQ2: Restrictions on adaptation.} \emph{Rank:} LoRA-16 beats LoRA-4 by 2.6\pp{} on GSM8K (paired 95\% CI [0.5, 4.8]) and 2.8\pp{} on BoolQ, with the same HellaSwag and ARC-Easy and a lower final SFT loss (0.199 vs.\ 0.211, mean of the last 50 updates). \emph{L2-SP:} the penalty cuts the final displacement $\|\theta-\theta_0\|_2$ from 10.64 to 6.28 ($-41\%$), but the SFT loss ends at the same value (0.184). At the end $\lambda\Omega=0.0197$, about 11\% of that loss. The math gain does not change ($+0.8$\pp{} vs.\ Full FT, $p=0.42$), HellaSwag differs by only 0.3\pp{} ($p=0.08$), and BoolQ goes up by 8.6\pp. Full FT's own drift is mostly early (10.01 by the midpoint). \emph{Orthogonal protection:} OPLoRA-16 matches LoRA-16 on GSM8K, BoolQ and ARC-Easy ($|\Delta|\le0.3$\pp, $p>0.3$) but is 0.31\pp{} lower on HellaSwag (95\% CI [$-0.55$, $-0.06$], $p=0.02$). That is the opposite of its purpose, though small and from one seed. \emph{Full FT vs.\ LoRA-16:} LoRA-16 gets 90\% of Full FT's GSM8K gain (the 2.1\pp{} gap has $p=0.08$) and keeps 1.1\pp{} more HellaSwag and 13.3\pp{} more BoolQ, matching the ``learns less, forgets less'' result of \citet{biderman2024lora}. But the two families use learning rates 10$\times$ apart, so I am really comparing recipes and can't credit the difference to the parameterization alone.

\paragraph{RQ3: Parameter changes and behavior.} Table~\ref{tab:t1diag} helps explain why orthogonal protection did so little. Without the constraint, updates do not prefer $W_0$'s top output directions: $\rho_k$ is 0.104--0.153 for Full FT, L2-SP and both LoRAs, close to the 0.125 a random direction would give. OPLoRA's constraint holds to numerical precision, so it only removes the roughly one-eighth of the update that would land there, and its scores stay close to LoRA-16's. All updates are small ($\eta$ of 0.7--1.9\%). LoRA-16 has a larger $\eta$ than Full FT at layers 0 and 13 but still keeps HellaSwag, so update size alone does not predict retention here. L2-SP lowers $\eta$ at all three layers (by 14--39\%). At layers 0 and 13 every update reacts more to math than to non-math inputs (layer-13 $R$ ratio 1.7--1.9 for full-parameter runs, 3.2--5.1 for adapters). At layer 27 this flips for every method except LoRA-4, and Full FT's correction is 2.9\% of the original output on non-math probes vs.\ 1.1--1.2\% for LoRA-16 and OPLoRA-16. Layer-27 $R_\text{non}$ is 2.5--2.9\% for full-parameter runs and 0.9--1.2\% for adapters, the same split as HellaSwag loss, but within the adapters the HellaSwag changes are mostly not significant, so this is a two-group pattern, not a ranking. $R$ also uses activations cached from the original model, so it ignores changes in earlier layers.

\paragraph{RQ4: Performance and computational cost.} The adapters train only 0.4--1.7\% of the parameters, use about a third of Full FT's peak memory and save weights 59--233$\times$ smaller (Table~\ref{tab:t1res}). Surprisingly, they were slower: with the same updates on the same GPU type, LoRA took 31\% longer than Full FT and OPLoRA-16 37\% longer than LoRA-16. My unprofiled guess is that at micro-batch 2 the extra small matrix products in 196 adapted modules (recomputed under checkpointing) cost more than the optimizer saves, and OPLoRA adds two projections per module. L2-SP needs a 2.27\,GB FP32 copy of $\theta_0$ (+2.2\,GB peak) and 105\,s of penalty computation, and OPLoRA stores 308\,MB of singular bases (SVD 16.9\,s). Overall, LoRA-16 gets most of the gain at near-minimal memory and storage, and L2-SP the biggest gain at full-model memory.

\paragraph{RQ5: Behavior beyond benchmark scores.} On the plain husband prompt (Table~\ref{tab:t1qual}), all three full-parameter checkpoints start with reasonable advice and then repeat until the 512-token cap (the midpoint counts from ``\#\#\#\# 1'' to ``\#\#\#\# 79''), while the adapters answer in a few sentences. MetaMathQA's answer format shows up in 2--4 of 6 unrelated replies for full-parameter checkpoints and in 0--1 for adapters; e.g.\ Full FT answers the wish prompt with ``\#\#\#\# 1 / The answer is: 1''. L2-SP was the best full-parameter run on the benchmarks, but it has the same artifacts as Full FT, so its benchmark advantage does not carry over to free-form replies. Only the baseline gives valid JSON for the husband JSON prompt. On the husband template, five of six fine-tuned checkpoints suggest ways to please the husband (``make him happy''), and the midpoint drifts to ``create a community garden''. From midpoint to final, Full FT's format artifacts go down (4 to 2) but loops go up (1 to 2). Nothing harmful came up. These are 42 greedy responses labelled by one non-blind annotator (me), so I treat them as examples, not rates.

\paragraph{RQ1: Learning a visual interface.} Stage~1 brings dev caption loss down from 5.09 to 2.10 (Fig.~\ref{fig:t2}). Even before any question training, the correct image already makes the reference answer more likely than the partner image: $\Delta_\text{NLL}$ goes from $-0.27$ to $+0.45$ on generated questions and from $0.22$ to $0.50$ on VQAv2. Correct-image answer NLL rises after update 500, though (generated: 2.31 to 3.52 by update 2{,}250), while no-image NLL stays at 5.33 since the language model is frozen. In Stage~2, correct-image NLL on generated questions falls to 0.55--0.71 and the swap penalty rises to 2.8--4.2. No-image NLL also falls (to 1.6--1.9), so part of what Stage~2 teaches is just the answer format and distribution, which need no image. Stage~3 lowers VQAv2 correct-image NLL from 1.53 to 1.02 and raises its swap penalty from 1.30 to 1.59, but the generated-question swap penalty falls from 4.18 to 2.83. So Stage~3 helps natural VQA and keeps caption loss flat (2.09--2.13), but loses some image discrimination learned on template questions, whose replay share drops to 20\%. Captioning was only measured through reference loss, since no captions were generated.

\paragraph{RQ2: Grounding and generalization.} The final model's answers depend on the image (Table~\ref{tab:t2}): the complementary image lowers accuracy by 54.7\pp{} on generated pairs and 27.7\pp{} on VQAv2 and raises reference NLL by 2.6 and 1.5 nats; all CIs exclude zero. The suites differ in question types and metric, so this gap is not purely generated vs.\ natural. Under the swap, 14.1\% of generated answers keep the original reference and 17.2\% match neither (VQAv2: 26.8\% and 18.7\%). Presence is 92.2\% correct with an 84.4\pp{} swap drop. Counting is 45.3\% correct vs.\ 31.2\% with no image, and ``How many donuts can you see?'' gets ``1'' in every condition even though one reference is ``2'' (Table~\ref{tab:t2q}). On VQAv2, number and other questions lose 31--42\pp{} under the swap, but yes/no questions lose only 14.9\pp. The yes/no result is weak anyway: always answering ``yes'' would already get 53.8\%, and the model gets 60.7\%.

\paragraph{RQ3: Measuring image use.} Teacher-forced NLL and greedy accuracy rank the four conditions the same way on both suites: correct, no image, mismatched, complementary (Table~\ref{tab:t2}). \paragraph{RQ1: Exploration and selection.} Sampling finds many more correct answers than selection picks (Fig.~\ref{fig:t3}, Table~\ref{tab:t3}). From $N{=}1$ to 32, oracle coverage goes from 64.3\% to 91.4\% on MATH and from 53.6\% to 89.5\% on MathVista, but SC only reaches 73.8\% and 61.7\%. Extra samples mostly repeat earlier answers: distinct answer groups only grow to 5.9 and 3.9 at $N{=}32$. In the failure breakdown for SC on MATH (Fig.~\ref{fig:app-t3b}), questions with no correct candidate drop from 35.6\% ($N{=}1$) to 8.6\% ($N{=}32$), and questions where a correct candidate existed but was not picked rise from 0 to 17.6\%. Missed correct candidates outnumber sampling failures from $N{=}16$ on MATH and from $N{=}8$ on MathVista, where at $N{=}32$ they are 27.8\% vs.\ 10.5\%.

\paragraph{RQ2: Evidence and consensus.} The signals carry some information. In questions with mixed correctness, the top quartile by self-certainty is correct 20.4\pp{} more often than the bottom quartile on MATH, vs.\ 13.4\pp{} for log-probability and 7.8\pp{} for being shorter. On MathVista the gaps are only 2.1--9.4\pp, largest for VisConf. But as an argmax, every score loses to plain voting at $N{=}32$ ($-3.2$ to $-13.4$\pp, all CIs below zero), and max log-probability gets worse as $N$ grows, down to 48.2\% on MathVista, below a single sample. Length does not explain this well. Within questions, $L$ has Spearman correlation $+0.22$ (MATH) and $+0.33$ (MathVista) with length, $K$ has $-0.16$ and $+0.35$, $V$ has $-0.69$, and VisConf has $-0.28$, yet $V$, the most length-tied, barely moves (54.5 to 53.8) while $L$ falls most. My guess is that the quartile gap measures average separation, but an argmax over more candidates picks the extreme tail, where confidently wrong rollouts win. Adding agreement through RWC fixes most of this. Self-certainty + RWC beats SC by 1.8\pp{} [0.7, 2.9] on MATH and VisConf + RWC by 1.6\pp{} [0.2, 3.1] on MathVista; the other MathVista RWC variants have CIs that include zero. RWC beats argmax for every score, but the best selector is still 15.9\pp{} (MATH) and 26.3\pp{} (MathVista) below oracle coverage at $N{=}32$. VisConf is clearly the best MathVista score with argmax (57.8 vs.\ at most 56.2) but only 0.2\pp{} ahead of $V$ with RWC. Confidence and visual engagement are negatively correlated within rollouts (mean $r=-0.21$), about equally for correct ($-0.20$) and incorrect ($-0.22$) ones, so this does not separate right from wrong. Token-wise fusion, which differs from VisConf by $\mathrm{Cov}_t(c,v)$ (identity checked to $2\times10^{-8}$), is slightly worse with both rules. In Fig.~\ref{fig:app-t3c}, on MathVista question 379 max-VisConf picks a short (116-token) wrong rollout with high mean $v$ (0.055) and max-self-certainty a 330-token correct one ($v=0.031$). On MATH \texttt{test:2460}, the first held-out question where SC and $K$+RWC disagree, ``58'' and the correct ``154'' tie at 11 of 32 votes and only $K$+RWC picks ``154''. None of these signals tell us whether the reasoning actually used the image.

\paragraph{RQ3: Breadth and refinement.} With the same 3{,}072-token output allowance, the single long response was the most accurate (Table~\ref{tab:t3live}): 68\%, vs.\ 54\% for three parallel responses (paired difference $-14$\pp{} [$-26$, $-2$]) and 60\% for refinement ($-8$\pp{} [$-20$, $+4$]). Truncation explains a lot of the parallel result: 16.7\% of parallel calls hit the 1{,}024-token cap and only 78\% of parallel runs end with an answer we can extract. All three of refinement's wrong-to-correct cases started from a first response that was cut off before giving an answer, and the revision supplied one. In the one correct-to-wrong case (\texttt{test:1324}), a correct $\tfrac{3}{16}$ was changed to $\tfrac{3}{32}$. So in this run revision mostly helped by finishing cut-off solutions, and it took 1.8$\times$ the time of the single response.

\paragraph{RQ4: Adaptive allocation.} Stopping once the revealed votes agree saves most of the cost of 32-sample voting with little accuracy loss (Table~\ref{tab:t3live}, right; Fig.~\ref{fig:app-t3a}). The agreement controller chosen on the dev split uses 52.5\% fewer tokens on MATH ($\Delta=-0.15$\pp{} [$-0.5$, $+0.2$]) and 63\% fewer on MathVista ($-0.4$\pp{} [$-1.4$, $+0.7$]). It also beats fixed budgets of similar cost: on MATH, 73.6\% at 10{,}140 tokens vs.\ 73.1\% for SC@16 at 10{,}678; on MathVista, 61.3\% at 3{,}289 vs.\ 60.9\% for SC@16 at 4{,}462. Looking back, it uses 4.0--4.4 rollouts when over 75\% of the pool is correct, but 22--26 on MATH (11--23 on MathVista) when at most half is, where its accuracy is 0--77\% (0--49\%). The quality-aware controller did not do better than agreement alone. Dev selection chose $\gamma=0$ on both datasets, which makes the margin condition empty, so this controller only adds the requirement that the SC and RWC winners agree, plus a lower $\tau$ on MATH (0.5 vs.\ 0.625). On MATH it was cheaper but 0.6\pp{} below fixed-32 SC (CI $[-1.2, -0.06]$). Sampling all 32 would not have fixed most wrong early stops: only 51 of 660 such runs on MATH (MathVista: 312 of 1{,}735) end with a correct fixed-32 SC answer. In \texttt{test:2460} the agreement controller can stop after four rollouts on a 3:1 vote for the wrong ``58'', even though the full pool ties 11:11.

\paragraph{RQ5: Compute and reliability.} Fixed-$N$ voting has falling returns: SC gains 2.6\pp{} from 4 to 8 samples on MATH but only 2.0\pp{} more from 8 to 32, for four times the tokens. Agreement stopping gave the best accuracy--cost trade-off I found, and RWC adds 1.6--1.8\pp{} but needs every rollout scored, a cost (like prefill and VisConf's attention collection) that token counts skip. The failures show the limits: at $N{=}32$ most errors are correct candidates that were not picked, argmax scores reward confidently wrong outliers, and splitting a live budget into short calls loses answers to truncation.

\section{Conclusion}
Changing the form of the update helped retention more than penalizing its size: the adapters reached 77--90\% of Full FT's GSM8K gain with no significant HellaSwag loss and no looping, while L2-SP kept the full gain and recovered BoolQ but its free-form replies did not improve. OPLoRA's constraint held but did not help, since normal updates were not concentrated in the protected directions anyway. The VLM's answers follow the image, but counting and yes/no questions gain least, and 44\% of natural pairs get the same answer for both images. With repeated sampling, coverage grew much faster than selection accuracy, confidence scores only helped together with voting, and agreement-based stopping gave the clearest saving. All of this is based on single seeds, small evaluation sets in Tasks~2--3 and correlational diagnostics, and Task~3's two datasets differ in both model and data.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Prompt format.} Each prompt is the chat template of the user turn with the non-thinking generation prefix (\texttt{<|im\_start|>assistant\textbackslash n<think>\textbackslash n\textbackslash n</think>\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}); the target is the response followed by \texttt{<|im\_end|>}. Only target tokens receive loss. The prefix is identical to the one used at evaluation, and I checked it against the template's rendering of a full conversation.
\item \textbf{Loss.} Summed token cross-entropy divided by the number of supervised tokens in the whole effective batch, so every token has equal weight regardless of how examples fall into micro-batches. The LM head is applied only at supervised positions.
\item \textbf{Schedule.} Hugging Face cosine schedule with warmup. It starts at LR 0, so update 1 makes no parameter change.
\item \textbf{L2-SP.} The penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta-\theta_0\|^2$ covers all parameters. Its gradient $\lambda(\theta-\theta_0)$ is added after unscaling and before clipping, so clipping applies to the full objective. $\theta_0$ is an FP32 copy on the GPU, and its memory and time are reported as overhead. AdamW's decoupled weight decay (0.01) stays active in this run as in all others, so L2-SP acts on top of it. The ratio $\lambda\Omega$/SFT loss is 0.107 against the last-50-update mean SFT loss and 0.120 against the final update's loss.
\item \textbf{LoRA and OPLoRA.} Implemented from scratch (no PEFT). $A$ uses Kaiming-uniform initialization ($a=\sqrt5$, as in PEFT) and $B=0$. Initialization is seeded per module name, so LoRA-16 and OPLoRA-16 begin from identical factors. OPLoRA computes the top-$k$ left and right singular vectors $U_k,V_k$ of each frozen $W_0$ once in FP32 and uses projected factors $\tilde B=(I-U_kU_k^\top)B$ and $\tilde A=A(I-V_kV_k^\top)$, so $\Delta W=\frac{\alpha}{r}\tilde B\tilde A$ is orthogonal to both subspaces. Projections run in FP32 outside autocast. On the exported factors the relative constraint residuals $\|U_k^\top\tilde B\|/\|\tilde B\|$ and $\|\tilde A V_k\|/\|\tilde A\|$ are at most $1.3\times10^{-4}$ and $1.9\times10^{-4}$, and $\rho_k$ of the trained update is below $10^{-7}$ at every diagnosed layer.
\item \textbf{Export check.} Adapters are merged into FP16 weights ($W_0+\Delta W$ computed in FP32) for evaluation. On 107 token positions, merged and unmerged logits differ by at most 0.14 (mean 0.007--0.008) for LoRA-4, LoRA-16 and OPLoRA-16, which is at FP16 rounding level, and the argmax token agrees at every position. The OPLoRA constraint is re-verified on the exported factors (\texttt{task1/export/}).
\item \textbf{Checkpoints and restarts.} Checkpoints are written at updates 312, 625, 937 and 1{,}250, and the weights at 625 and 1{,}250 are kept for evaluation. Only the newest checkpoint keeps optimizer state. Resuming from a checkpoint reproduces uninterrupted training exactly. Four runs (all except LoRA-16) were resumed once after a session interruption. LoRA-4 and LoRA-16 were also restarted from scratch before their first checkpoint, and only the final attempt's log is kept (\texttt{task1/runs/log\_cleaning.json}).
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} Primary metrics are flexible-extract exact match for GSM8K, \texttt{acc\_norm} for HellaSwag and ARC-Easy, and \texttt{acc} for BoolQ. Because lm-eval does not pass \texttt{enable\_thinking} to the chat template, a small subclass renders the template with thinking disabled, and the rendered GSM8K prompt is asserted to contain the empty think block. GSM8K uses the task's stock stop sequences and filters with a fixed generation batch of 64; likelihood tasks use no chat template. Strict match requires the exact phrase ``The answer is $N$.'' and is 0 for every condition. The baseline ends its solutions in other formats (``\textbf{Final Answer:} \$18'', ``\textbackslash boxed\{4\}'', ``\textbf{Answer:} 100 minutes''), and the fine-tuned models write ``The answer is: $N$'' with a colon. BoolQ ``yes'' rates are computed from the saved per-example log-likelihoods.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics.} $\rho_k=\|U_kU_k^\top\Delta W\|_F^2/\|\Delta W\|_F^2$ (random baseline $k/d_{\text{out}}=0.125$); the analogous input-side fraction is in the CSV. $R(z)=\|\Delta W z\|_2/(\|W_0z\|_2+10^{-8})$ uses the \texttt{down\_proj} input $z$ at the last prompt token, cached once from the starting model in FP32. The 64 probes were sampled before any result was seen (\texttt{task1/probe\_set.json}).
\item \textbf{Qualitative annotation rubric.} All 168 greedy responses are saved grouped by question and format (\texttt{task1/qualitative/all\_responses\_grouped.json}). For the 42 annotated responses I record \emph{format compliance} as yes/partial/no for JSON and template prompts (partial = fields filled but extra text, unfilled placeholders, or JSON only appended after prose; a fenced JSON block counts as compliant); \emph{coherence/relevance} on a 0--2 scale (2 = answers the actual question, 1 = fluent but off-target, such as a role reversal or reframed question, or relevant then degenerate, 0 = repeats the prompt or is off-topic); \emph{repetition} as none/some/heavy (heavy = loop to the token cap); \emph{math framing} and \emph{inappropriate content} as yes/no; and a supporting excerpt. Automatic flags were used only as checks. The keyword-based \texttt{math\_markers} flag misses ``The answer is:'' and ``\#\#\#\#'', so it is not reported.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Visual features.} SigLIP's \texttt{last\_hidden\_state} was checked to equal the post-LayerNorm encoder output, so LayerNorm is applied exactly once. Images are resized to 224 (bicubic), rescaled by 1/255 and normalized with mean and std 0.5.
\item \textbf{Prompt.} \texttt{<|vision\_start|>}, 196 \texttt{<|image\_pad|>} tokens and \texttt{<|vision\_end|>} precede the text in the user turn, and thinking is disabled. Captioning uses ``Write a short caption for this image.'', and questions append the short-answer instruction ``Answer the question using a single word or phrase.''. For the no-image condition the whole vision block is removed and positions are recomputed. Prompts are identical across image conditions. Length caps: question 80, caption 64, answer 16 tokens.
\item \textbf{Loss.} Token-mean NLL within each example, averaged over examples in the effective batch.
\item \textbf{Data.} Captions are the first three captions (by annotation ID) of 6{,}000 train2014 images; 96 val2014 images form the caption dev set. Generated questions use COCO instances. A presence positive needs a non-crowd instance covering at least 0.5\% of the image, a negative has no annotation of the category, and counting images have 1--4 instances, all non-crowd and each at least 0.5\% of the image. Training draws from pre-generated pools of 20{,}000 presence and 20{,}000 counting pairs over training images. VQAv2 pairs require at least 8 of 10 annotators to agree and both questions to share an answer type; the target is the most frequent original string in the consensus group. Development and test images come from val2014, and all five evaluation sets use mutually disjoint images (528 in total; 8{,}730 training images).
\item \textbf{Sampling.} Stage 1 makes two shuffled passes over the captions. In Stages 2--3 each draw selects a component by its mixture weight and contributes a full question pair or two captions, so both members of a complementary pair are always in the same batch.
\item \textbf{Interface checks.} At the start of each stage, no backbone parameter requires or receives a gradient, and the connector (and LoRA, from Stage 2) receives finite non-zero gradients (\texttt{task2/vis196/summary.json}). Assertions during data preparation checked split isolation.
\item \textbf{Connector scale.} Fixed at initialization as the mean text-embedding norm (0.870) divided by the mean connector-output norm (15.87) on 256 training examples, which gives 0.0549. It is not trained (\texttt{task2/vis196/calibration.json}).
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} Greedy decoding with a 16-token cap and batch size 8. VQA answers use the official normalization and soft accuracy, ported from the VQA evaluation script; normalization is also applied when all ten answers are identical. Mismatched images come from a fixed seeded derangement within each suite (saved). The partner-follow rate scores each answer against the partner question's reference. The swap outcome split and the constant-answer yes/no scores are in \texttt{task2/vis196/report/extra\_stats.json}. Checkpoints were never selected using test results; the final Stage~3 model is evaluated.
\item \textbf{Optional 49-token run.} Same recipe with 2$\times$2 average pooling of the feature grid before a newly initialized connector. Training took 44.3\,min (vs.\ 45.7) at 1.5\,GB peak (vs.\ 1.6). Generated-question accuracy was 72.7\% (swap advantage 60.2\pp) vs.\ 68.8\% (54.7\pp), VQAv2 47.0\% (16.7\pp) vs.\ 54.5\% (27.7\pp), and answers took 21.8/25.3\,ms vs.\ 23.1/26.2 (\texttt{task2/vis196/report/compression.md}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Task 3}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Grouping and grading.} Equivalence is a symmetric math-verify 0.9.0 comparison without gold answers (cached, with timeouts). MathVista multiple-choice answers are mapped to option letters using the released metadata. All unparseable answers form one \textsc{no\_answer} group, which is graded incorrect and kept in voting, cost and accuracy. Correctness is used only for evaluation and retrospective analysis, never for selection. The supplied README counts (rows, questions, retained tokens, capped rollouts, null extractions) are reproduced exactly (\texttt{task3/*/data\_checks.json}).
\item \textbf{Splits and orders.} Sorted question IDs are permuted with \texttt{default\_rng(6304)}. The first 200 form the development split and the rest the held-out split; the live run uses the first 50 held-out MATH questions. All methods and stopping policies share ten seeded rollout orders per question. Results average over orders within a question, then over questions.
\item \textbf{Selection.} Score and vote ties go to the earliest supporting rollout in the current order. RWC weights the candidate ranked $r$ (average ranks for ties) by $(N-r)/(N-1)$ and sums weights within answer groups. VisConf's 5th and 95th KL percentiles are recomputed on each revealed candidate set with linear interpolation. Missing attention denominators are excluded from the visual score. A shortest-rollout selector serves as a length-only control.
\item \textbf{Cost.} Generated-token cost is the supplied retained token count (\texttt{n\_generated\_text\_tokens}), summed over consumed candidates; FirstAns consumes one.
\item \textbf{Adaptive stopping.} After 4, 8 and 16 revealed rollouts, the agreement controller stops when a unique non-\textsc{no\_answer} leading answer has vote share $\ge\tau$, $\tau\in\{0.5,0.625,0.75,0.875,1\}$. The quality-aware controller also requires the RWC winner (self-certainty for MATH, VisConf for MathVista) to agree and its margin to be at least $\gamma\in\{0,0.1,0.25,0.5\}$. Otherwise it uses all 32. Both return the self-consistency answer at stopping. On the development split I choose the setting with the lowest mean cost whose accuracy is within 1\,pp of fixed-32 self-consistency (fixed-32 itself is always eligible), breaking ties by higher accuracy and then smaller $\tau$ and $\gamma$, and freeze it.
\item \textbf{Live run.} Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct at revision \texttt{aa8e7253}, FP16, batch size 1, temperature 0.7, top-$p$ 0.8, top-$k$ 20, repetition penalty 1.0, independently seeded calls (250 calls, 250 seeds), no gold feedback, on one Tesla T4. The refinement prompt asks the model to review the shared first response, correct any errors and keep a correct answer. The revised answer is returned even when it contains no answer (counted as \textsc{no\_answer}), with no fallback to the first answer. One call came from a short earlier session on the same GPU type, from which the background run then resumed. Transition sources were checked against \texttt{task3/live/calls.jsonl}.
\end{itemize}
